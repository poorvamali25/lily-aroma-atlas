The recurring core

Across studies, several molecules repeatedly occupy the center of the chemical picture — but their abundance and sensory relevance change by cultivar and condition.

β-Ocimene

A recurrent monoterpene and one of the strongest recurring headspace signals in scented cultivars.

Linalool

A major floral monoterpene in selected cultivars, with studied developmental and circadian regulation.

Methyl benzoate

An exceptionally widespread benzenoid in the 2019 accession set, with a well-studied biosynthetic route.

One rule governs the atlas

“Chemically detected” is not the same statement as “aroma-active,” and “aroma-active” is not the same statement as “causally responsible.”

The database therefore keeps OAV, GC-O, sensory correlation, reconstruction, and uncertainty as separate evidence axes.

80-person sensory experiment

An oriental lily cultivar (‘Sweetness’) was evaluated by an 80-person non-trained panel alongside headspace GC-MS. The study found that VOC abundance was not directly equivalent to human scent perception.

36-cultivar chemometrics

A 2024 study combined sensory evaluation, HS-SPME-GC-MS and multivariate statistics. It identified VOC patterns and statistical associations with aroma attributes across 36 varieties.

Association ≠ causation

Cross-sample relationships tell you what varies with a sensory attribute. They do not prove that adding or removing the compound will recreate the whole flower odor.

OAV compatibility

Threshold matrix, threshold type, units, temperature and concentration basis must be compatible before an OAV is computed.

Reconstruction

A robust lily-flower reconstruction / omission dataset was not verified in the current primary literature set.

Stereochemistry

Older floral VOC reports often do not resolve stereoisomers sufficiently for a universal stereochemical aroma model.

Biology Layer

The molecule is only the beginning.

Lily fragrance changes with tissue, development, time of day, light, temperature and genotype.

Tepals become the scent engine

In the best-studied Lilium ‘Siberia’ system, tepals were the main floral scent-emitting tissue. This is a biological context field, not a universal rule for every lily.

Flower opening changes emission

In ‘Siberia’, the full-flowering stage contained most of the identified scent compounds and 18 of 21 identified flower-scent compounds reached their maximum at full flowering.

The clock matters

Emission changed across 04:00, 10:00, 16:00 and 22:00. Individual compounds peaked at different times, so “day vs night” should not be stored as one universal rule.

Environment modulates the output

Light and temperature alter floral volatile emission. One controlled ‘Siberia’ study found an increase-then-decrease response over the tested temperature/light ranges, with approximately 30 °C giving a high emission state in that experiment.

Genes meet chemistry

Monoterpene synthases act on GPP in the MEP-derived branch. Methyl benzoate has a studied benzenoid pathway involving benzoic-acid methyltransferase activity, with additional candidate acyltransferase evidence in ‘Siberia’.

Methodology

Built for scientific separation.

Every layer carries its own meaning, because mixing them is how natural-aroma databases become misleading.

GC-MS

What is present?

Analytical identification and quantification establish chemical presence. They do not by themselves prove sensory importance.

Headspace

What is released?

Dynamic headspace and HS-SPME measurements focus on compounds that enter the gas phase under the collection conditions.

OAV

What is theoretically smellable?

OAV = concentration / odor threshold, but only when concentration and threshold contexts are compatible.

GC-O / AEDA

What reaches a human nose?

These methods add a direct olfactory layer and can locate potent odor-active regions.

Sensory

What do people perceive?

Panel descriptors, intensity, liking and chemometric relationships are stored separately from chemistry.

Reconstruction

What contributes?

Omission, addition and recombination experiments are stronger evidence for contribution than presence alone.

Evidence ladder

LEVEL A

Direct experimental sensory evidence: GC-O, AEDA, reconstruction, omission/addition, direct sensory experiments.

LEVEL B

Strong analytical + sensory support: compatible OAV, GC-O and consistent sensory descriptors.

LEVEL C

Chemical + threshold support: concentration known, compatible threshold, OAV calculated, without direct GC-O.

LEVEL D

Chemical presence only: analytically detected but individual sensory importance unknown.

LEVEL E

Indirect inference: related species, external databases or general chemical knowledge.

Data-integrity rule: missing evidence stays “not reported”, “not measured”, “unknown”, or “not applicable”. Empty fields are never backfilled by guesses.
